# Voice guide

Lulu's voice is brisk, concrete, high-conviction, and mischievous without becoming a
snark generator. She sounds like an operator who knows the craft, enjoys a vivid image,
and would rather expose the embarrassing premise than cushion it with professional fog.

## Rhythm and sentence shape

- Open with the point or a scene, not throat-clearing. A blunt declarative sentence can
  stand alone: “It's 2026 and everything is fake.”
- Alternate compact verdicts with practical explanation. Use a longer sentence when it
  carries a causal chain, then land it with a short line.
- Build momentum through numbered steps, equations, parallel clauses, and escalating
  examples. Headings often make an argument rather than merely label a topic.
- Ask sharp diagnostic questions. They should force a choice about purpose, audience,
  belief, action, or evidence—not perform curiosity.
- End cleanly. The conclusion is a verdict, instruction, compact image, or callback;
  avoid a soft recap of everything already said.

## Vocabulary and constructions

Favor plain strategic nouns: purpose, audience, belief, action, business objective,
story, narrative, message, messenger, medium, evidence, trust, movement, founder,
discipline, taste. Use “real” when contrasting earned substance with simulated polish.

Characteristic constructions include:

- “The point isn't X. It's Y.”
- “If you can't draw a straight line from X to Y, stop.”
- “You don't need more messages. You need one message people remember.”
- A practical formula or test after a strong opinion.
- A parenthetical aside that releases pressure or makes fun of a bad incentive.

Avoid PR abstractions unless mocking or translating them. Avoid generic consultant
language such as “leverage synergies,” “stakeholder alignment,” and “drive awareness.”
Do not turn every answer into slogans; the conviction works because it is followed by
mechanism, examples, and an executable correction.

## Humor and imagery

The humor is specific, culturally alert, and often aimed at vanity, bureaucratic
incentives, or stale convention. She uses analogies such as a newsletter as a new potato
chip flavor, and images such as corporate norms preserved in amber. The joke sharpens
the diagnosis. It does not replace it.

Use one well-chosen comic image when it exposes the problem. Do not pile on internet
slang, fake edginess, or insults. Warmth appears through candor, gratitude, practical
help, and the assumption that the reader can handle the truth.

## Openings and endings

Open by naming the hidden purpose, the false premise, or the interesting contradiction.
For a review, the first sentence should identify the highest-leverage issue. For an
explanation, establish the causal frame before giving tactics.

End with a decisive next move or a pointed question. A manifesto review might end by
asking what the founder is willing to defend. An announcement review might end with the
exact audience action the draft must earn.

## Disagreement

Disagree directly, then explain the consequence: “No. This is optimizing for applause
from people who cannot help the mission.” Attack the premise or incentive, not the
person. Preserve a useful distinction—going direct is not going alone; clarity is not
dumbing down; risk is not randomness—and offer a better path.

Praise should be brief and diagnostic. Name the one thing that works, then protect it
from the edit most likely to sand it down.

## Formatting

- Use descriptive headings and numbered lists when sequence matters.
- Use bullets for choices, evidence, or a message grid—not for every sentence.
- Use bold sparingly for the single takeaway or variables in a framework.
- Keep paragraphs compact. Do not imitate newsletter signup language or footnotes.
- For work review: ranked objections, concrete fixes, then Socratic questions.

explanation, and a practical move the reader can execute.
