# Lulu Cheng Meservey: taste profile

## How to read this profile

**Direct** marks an idea Lulu states in the cited body of work. **Synthesis** marks a
review rule inferred by combining those statements. Synthesis is deliberately
specific enough to guide work, but it must never be presented as a quotation or as
an exact recommendation Lulu has published.

## Manifestos and founder communications first

Lulu treats communication as part of building the company, not as decorative copy
applied after the real work. The founder possesses the secret knowledge, conviction,
and appetite for conflict that make a mission distinct. Helpers can improve execution,
but they should not replace the founder as author of the story.

- **Direct — purpose before prose.** Communications should serve a business purpose.
  Start with the end, not the channel, asset, or hoped-for view count.
- **Synthesis — match the call to action to the purpose.** A manifesto can serve
  identity and positioning without a literal call to action; an operational
  announcement normally needs the audience to do something observable.
- **Direct — solve backward from belief.** Identify the business objective, the action
  needed, the people who can take it, and the belief that would make the action feel
  right. Only then choose the message, messenger, medium, and tactic.
- **Direct — the founder owns the story.** Going direct means telling your own story
  without depending on intermediaries. It does not mean refusing skilled help or
  ignoring useful amplification.
- **Direct — narrative has an arc.** Strong stories move from the problem through its
  resolution: how we got here, what is missing, what is possible, and how the company
  reaches the promised land.
- **Direct — one message must survive.** Decide the single idea the audience should
  remember. Put it early. Do not make the audience excavate the point from context.
- **Synthesis — a manifesto is a compact theory of change.** It should name the broken
  convention, explain why the old world no longer works, establish who must act, show
  the better world, and make allegiance emotionally available. Its job is not to list
  features or satisfy every stakeholder.

## Worldview

### Earn the right to attention

- **Direct.** No company is owed attention, affection, or favorable coverage. Strategy
  improves the odds; it does not guarantee an outcome.
- **Direct.** Quality, dignity, and taste are worth more than empty virality. Durable
  narratives are built through consistent effort over months, not one engagement spike.
- **Direct.** Real work is now narrative advantage: real outcomes, evidence, physical
  artifacts, relationships, human flaws, discipline, and craft cut through synthetic slop.
- **Synthesis.** If the underlying act is generic, prose cannot rescue it. Search for a
  real decision, sacrifice, result, artifact, or tension before manufacturing content.

### Distinctiveness comes from ownership

- **Direct.** A person or company should be number one at its own thing, not a weaker
  version of an established voice. Edges, oddity, and conviction create memorability.
- **Direct.** Faceless committees sand passion into generic corporate language. A draft
  that is imperfect but recognizably yours can beat polished work that could belong to
  any company.
- **Synthesis.** Do not confuse voice with ornamental quirk. Distinctiveness must reveal
  a real worldview, an unusual choice, or a stake the speaker is willing to defend.

### Build a direct relationship

- **Direct.** Founders should build their own platform, audience, and narrative. Email
  lists and direct channels reduce dependence on changing gatekeepers.
- **Direct.** Going direct is not synonymous with posting more or using one social
  network. Third parties still matter when they add credible evidence or amplification.
- **Synthesis.** Judge a channel by whether it reaches the actual audience with the
  right messenger and proof, not by prestige or fashion.

## Problem selection

Lulu is most interested when communications can create business value, preserve a
mission, recruit believers, move product, win trust, or build a movement. She looks for
high-leverage narrative problems: a misunderstood technology, a founder with unusually
strong conviction, an earned crisis response, a launch that needs a master narrative,
or a community that can express itself through the story.

She has little patience for isolated comms goals, prestige channels selected by habit,
committee appeasement, metrics with no line to the business, or virality as ego care.
The key diagnostic is causal: which audience belief changes which action, and how does
that action serve the mission?

## Quality bar

### Clarity a second-grader could carry

- **Direct.** Make the central message understandable to a second-grader. Simplicity is
  the product of clear thought, not condescension.
- **Direct.** Use clear, decisive language in a crisis. In any format, end thoughts
  cleanly and avoid verbal fog.
- **Synthesis.** A technically accurate sentence still fails when only an insider can
  explain why it matters. Translate the mechanism and preserve the stakes.

### Evidence beats adjectives

- **Direct.** Anecdotes, stories, statistics, credible reports, advocates, studies, and
  customer experience make a point more memorable and believable than words such as
  “innovative,” “great,” or “disruptive.”
- **Direct.** Real outcomes and real effort matter more than flashy claims. Third-party
  evidence is especially useful when trust is the belief that must change.
- **Synthesis.** Circle each evaluative adjective. Replace it with an observable fact,
  a scene, a number with context, a named artifact, or a credible outside witness.

### Memorable shape, not just correct information

- **Direct.** A story needs a problem and a resolution. A message needs one dominant
  takeaway. Audience motivations and cultural preoccupations determine the bridge.
- **Direct.** The most powerful memes let people express something about themselves.
- **Synthesis.** Create imagery and use unusual ordering when they make the argument
  easier to remember: a concrete opening scene, a surprising enemy, or the promised
  land before the implementation detail. Novelty without strategic purpose is noise.

### Human enough to trust

- **Direct.** Ultra-polished personas and generic scripts read as fake. Real flaws,
  foibles, effort, and outcomes make the speaker credible.
- **Synthesis.** Leave in the human specificity: the hard choice, embarrassing mistake,
  cost, constraint, or unfinished edge. Do not manufacture vulnerability as a tactic.

## Method

1. **Direct:** Name no more than a few real business ends.
2. **Direct:** Work backward through action, audience, and belief.
3. **Direct:** Build the master narrative and the grid of key messages.
4. **Direct:** Choose the best messengers and the mediums the audience actually uses.
5. **Direct:** Distill one headline message and make it appear immediately.
6. **Direct:** Supply anecdotes, numbers, artifacts, and third-party credibility.
7. **Synthesis:** Pressure-test the draft for founder ownership, sharp edges, human
   reality, and whether someone could recognize the speaker without seeing the byline.
8. **Direct:** Execute with discipline, learn from response, and build the narrative
   consistently over time rather than changing identity for each engagement cycle.

## Manifesto drafting and review rubric — synthesis

This rubric is a practical synthesis of Lulu's published communications principles,
not a checklist she has published.

1. **Purpose:** What durable identity, positioning, or business end does this serve?
2. **Audience:** Who specifically matters, what do they already care about, and who can
   be ignored even if they are loud?
3. **Belief and action:** What one belief must change? Is action required? A positioning
   manifesto may deliberately omit a CTA; a launch, apology, or operational notice
   should make the next action unmistakable.
4. **Enemy or failure:** What convention, constraint, or missing thing created the
   present problem? Is it concrete enough to oppose rather than vague “change”?
5. **Arc:** Does the piece show how we got here, what is missing, what becomes possible,
   and the promised land the speaker is building toward?
6. **Founder truth:** Could this only have come from the person with secret knowledge
   of the project? Restore the firsthand insight, conviction, and risk.
7. **Single message:** Can a reader repeat one sentence tomorrow? Move it earlier and
   demote anything competing with it.
8. **Clarity:** Could a second-grader understand the causal story without knowing the
   jargon? Simplify structure before merely shortening words.
9. **Proof:** Replace adjectives with anecdotes, statistics, real outcomes, artifacts,
   customer evidence, or credible third parties.
10. **Identity:** Does sharing the piece let believers say something true about
    themselves? Give the audience a banner, not just information.
11. **Edges:** What does the piece risk? Restore the unusual ordering, image, claim, or
    choice that a committee would delete—provided it serves the purpose.
12. **Durability:** Can this claim be reinforced by real work for 6–18 months? If not,
    narrow the promise or change the behavior behind it.

## Pet peeves

- Random tactics with no causal line to a business outcome.
- “Go viral,” impressions, share of voice, and other prestige or ego metrics as ends.
- Writing for the loudest critics instead of the audience that matters.
- Generic corporate prose produced by consensus and stripped of conviction.
- Several competing messages where one memorable message is required.
- Adjectives doing the work that evidence, stories, or statistics should do.
- Scripts polished beyond human recognition; fake testimonials and vague claims.
- Outsourcing story and strategy, even when outsourcing execution is sensible.
- Playing safe until the work becomes interchangeable; chasing engagement at the cost
  of quality, dignity, or taste.
- Treating one launch as a narrative when consistency and real work are missing.

## Influences and traditions

**Direct.** Her writing explicitly draws from military strategy (ends, ways, means and
decisive execution), classical rhetoric and Roman civic symbolism (the Rostra), science
fiction (Asimov's *Foundation*), and movement-building. Her examples favor founders,
builders, propagandists in the neutral sense of spreading an idea, and speakers willing
to own a position.

**Synthesis.** The resulting tradition is less “PR polish” than founder-led rhetoric:
strategy before tactics, argument embodied in a person, memorable story backed by proof,
and direct audience ownership sustained long enough to become reputation.
