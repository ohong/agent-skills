# Finalization: Diligence, Decision, Lock-in, Stewardship

Load this file for deeper diligence on finalists (Phase 4.3+), the decision itself (Phase 5), and post-decision guidance (Phase 6). Source: guide §4.3–6 (PDF pp. 24–31), domain appendix A.4 (PDF p. 39).

## Deeper diligence on finalists (§4.3, PDF pp. 24–25)

### Trademark (§4.3.1)
A trademark isn't owning a word — it's the exclusive right to use it for specific goods/services, *to the extent it's distinctive*. Enforceability is the whole game: generic or descriptive terms ("Energy" for batteries) can't be defended. Distinctiveness spectrum, weakest → strongest: **generic → descriptive → suggestive → arbitrary → fanciful/invented**. Aim suggestive or better.

Process: quick knock-out search on TMView (filter by class and jurisdiction) yourself; after settling on a final candidate, hire counsel for a comprehensive federal/state/common-law search yielding a Green (clear) / Yellow (some risk) / Red (high conflict) opinion. Then decide whether the name's strength outweighs the risk.

### Domain strategy (§4.3.2 + A.4, PDF pp. 24–25, 39)
The exact .com is nice, not essential — Placek calls it "just an area code," and Square ran on squareup.com for years while square.com belonged to Square Enix. **Never sacrifice seed capital for domain purity, and never let domain availability drive the name choice.** Premium domains run six to seven figures through opaque brokers; a decent negotiated purchase is often $15–30K if you want it later.

Unlock ladder (try in order):
1. **Exact match, alternate TLD** — .net (underrated: early-internet credentials, affordable), .ai, .dev, .io. Prefer specific TLDs over .co, which reads as a .com fallback.
2. **Category TLDs** — .studio, .art (creative); .vc, .capital, .fund (finance); .tech, .cloud.
3. **Modifiers** — get-, try-, join-, hello- prefixes; -hq, -app, -labs suffixes; wearename, teamname, madebyname.
4. **Word variants** — drop vowels, tweak spelling, double the word, abbreviate.
5. **Domain hacks** — split the word across the TLD; rare TLDs (.one, .run, .world).

Social handles follow the same logic: an exact handle matters in fashion/beauty/lifestyle; in SaaS/B2B, memorability of the brand beats the handle. If a domain-availability tool is connected, check finalists as part of diligence — but report availability as information, not verdict.

### International / cultural review (§4.3.3)
For multi-market ambitions, always get a native-speaker check. Two risks: pronunciation difficulty, and unintended meanings/slang. Automated translation won't catch nuance — ask people from those cultures directly.

### Mockups (§4.4, PDF p. 25)
Names behave differently in the wild. Drop finalists into a headline, an app icon, a social handle, an email signature. Render in ALL CAPS / lowercase / mixed and several fonts — GOOGLE, Google, google carry different personalities. A strong name stays legible and consistent from billboard to favicon. (As an agent, produce these mockups — it's cheap for you and it surfaces "unknown knowns" the user can only discover by seeing.)

### Future-proofing (§4.5, PDF p. 26)
Pair the name with plausible future product names — does it extend into a family? Does it yield a natural demonym (Google → Googlers)? Does anything about it scream *right now* (of-the-moment memes, The Thing Company of Place copycats)?

## The decision (§5.1, PDF pp. 27–28)

**One person decides.** Committees find the safe middle; you want memorable. The final decision-maker must have been present for Orientation. Clarity beats consensus — the person living with the name for ten years makes the call. (You wouldn't let the doctor name your kid; don't let consultants — or the agent — make the final call either.)

Two supported methods:

1. **Phone-wallpaper test (preferred)**: set each finalist, in clean text, as the phone background for 3–4 days. Hundreds of low-pressure exposures reveal what conference rooms can't. One option starts feeling obviously right.
2. **Structured meeting**: 45 minutes hard cap, decision-makers only. For each finalist someone argues for and someone against (no advocate → easy cut). Secret ballot. Ties → CEO veto.

**Don't wait for a eureka.** Across Lexicon's ~4,000 projects, clients essentially never had an instant aha moment. Comfort is the wrong test — see `masters.md` for handling a team that finds the best candidate uncomfortable.

### Decision brief (assemble per finalist, §5.1, PDF pp. 27–28)
- **User evidence** — best expectation-setting quote from testing; ranking performance; concerns and their weight
- **Legal status** — counsel's Green/Yellow/Red; filing strategy; risks
- **Domain & social** — availability, cost, fallback strategy
- **International** — language/cultural findings, pronunciation
- **Future flexibility** — one concrete expansion it accommodates; limits it creates
- **Case for** — 2–3 strongest arguments
- **Case against** — honest weaknesses; why you might regret it

## Lock-in — same day (§5.2, PDF p. 28)

Doubt is the enemy of decision; make it official before buyer's remorse kicks in:
1. File the trademark application within 24 hours
2. Purchase domains or engage a broker
3. Secure social handles on all major platforms
4. Draft the internal name story (see Stewardship)

## Handling regret (§5.2.1, PDF p. 29)

Post-decision doubt is universal and predictable — once committed, the brain hunts for problems. Counsel the user with these truths:

- They didn't pick at random — trust the process they ran.
- No name is perfect: Apple was "too simple," Google "too silly," Amazon "too long." Placek's own hits were hated at birth — Azure was called dumb, Sonos got rejected as not entertainment enough, BlackBerry's founders thought it crazy.
- The brand built around the name is what makes it powerful; names accrue meaning, they don't start with it.
- Every romanticized alternative had flaws that have simply been forgotten.
- Live with it for a few weeks in real situations before considering a change.
- Get specific about the concern — naming anxiety is often displaced anxiety about the company itself.

## Stewardship (§6, PDF pp. 30–31)

The name is 20% of the journey; the other 80% is what happens next.

- **Craft the founding myth** — the story of how the name came to be becomes brand lore; employees retell it, customers repeat it.
- **Use it with total consistency** — no hedging, no explanatory taglines, no apologetic introductions. Confidence makes names work.
- **Build a name family** — product and feature names should show family resemblance, not accumulate as random words.
- **Protect it** — actively police the trademark; keep demonyms natural, not forced.
- **Let it compound** — names deepen with repetition. Nike means victory by decades of triumph, not just etymology; Amazon once puzzled and now reads as the everything store. You don't need the perfect name, only one used with conviction.
