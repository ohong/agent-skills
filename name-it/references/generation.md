# Generation: Producing Name Candidates in Volume

Load this file during Exploration (Phase 2). Source: guide §2.1–2.6 (PDF pp. 16–19) and the name taxonomy in appendix A.2 (PDF pp. 32–37).

## The volume doctrine

The winning name rarely comes from the first brainstorm — it comes from "session seven," when the obvious choices are exhausted and you finally get weird. Placek's Lexicon generates 1,000–1,500 candidates per project and expects ~90% to die in filtering. Generate first, judge later: premature evaluation kills originality. As an agent, generate far more names internally than you show — work through every technique below, then present only curated batches.

Target for an agent session: 100+ internal candidates spanning at least six name types before your first presentation to the user.

## Techniques (work through all of them)

### 1. Free naming (§2.1, PDF p. 17)
Dump the obvious ideas first — descriptive, trendy, even bad ones. This clears the reflexive layer and gives raw material for springboards. Nothing here is final.

### 2. Bad names first (A Hundred Monkeys)
Deliberately write ten terrible names for this exact product. Articulate *why* each fails (generic, try-hard, wrong tone, competitor-clone, dated trope). Inverting the failure modes sharpens what "good" means for this brief.

### 3. Springboards (§2.2, PDF p. 17)
Escalate orientation words: word → broader theme → specific real-world reference → candidate. Push past descriptive into evocative — real things have three-dimensionality you can hang meaning on.

- Calm → Sleep → Night Sky → **Orion**
- Futuristic → SciFi → Famous authors → **Asimov**
- Elegant affection → Refined languages (French) → **Câlin** (to cuddle)
- Relaxed → Pillow → **Cool Side**

### 4. Meaningful facets (§2.3, PDF p. 18)
List associations with the category, product mechanics, founding story, locations, books, characters, historical figures — then research them for insider vocabulary. History books on the subject have richer language than startup blogs. Great names found this way:

- **A24** — the Italian highway the founder was driving when the idea struck
- **Stripe** — the magnetic stripe on credit cards, for a company moving payments online
- **Cisco** — the tail end of San Francisco, its hometown
- **Palantir** — Tolkien's seeing stone, matching the company's mysterious air

Prompts: What would an insider know? What's one degree away from this concept? What's the emotional wavelength of this theme?

### 5. Lexical rabbitholing (§2.4, PDF p. 18)
Follow daisy-chains through etymology, obscure and specialist dictionaries, foreign languages, and Wikipedia. Get beyond Greek gods and startup tropes. Onym.co catalogs source dictionaries and tools.

### 6. Oblique briefs (Lexicon's method)
Lexicon runs parallel small teams where only one knows the real brief — another names a "competitor," a third names something unrelated (a bicycle, not an AI IDE). This prevents category clichés. **Windsurf** came from a team brainstorming "flow" with no idea they were naming a code editor. As an agent, simulate this: run separate generation passes where you name an imaginary product in a *different* category that must evoke the same dominant feeling, then harvest the transferable candidates.

### 7. Compounds — 1 + 1 = 3 (Lexicon)
Two known words fused create richer mental networks than either alone, and outperform their length in memorability: PowerBook, BlackBerry, Facebook, Windsurf. Pair a concrete noun from your facet list with a texture word from your feeling.

### 8. Sound-first generation (Lexicon sound symbolism)
Pick target phonemes for the intended feeling (see the sound-symbolism table in `evaluation.md`), then generate real and coined words containing them. E.g., wanting "alive and daring" → seed with V words; wanting "reliable" → B words.

### 9. Modification & hacks (§2.5, PDF p. 19)
Creative misspellings, portmanteaus, dropped vowels or added letters (careful — can read dot-com-bust), descriptive endings (see suffix list below).

## Name type taxonomy (appendix A.2, PDF pp. 32–37)

Cover multiple types; orientation usually narrows which ones fit. Full example lists are on the cited PDF pages.

| Type | What it is | Pros | Cons | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Descriptive** (p. 32–33) | Says what it does; includes acronyms, compounds, generic descriptors | Instantly clear | Hard to trademark or own; caps mystique; handicaps every conversation after the first | Dropbox, YouTube, LinkedIn, 7-Eleven, The Browser Company |
| **Experiential** (p. 34) | Conveys the outcome or feeling of use | Emotionally resonant; people buy feelings, not features | Can be abstract; may not age with product expansion | Slack, Zoom, Tinder, Red Bull, Seamless |
| **Evocative** (p. 35) | Familiar word transplanted into a surprising context | Hijacks existing imagery; feels inevitable once understood | Forced if the connection is weak | Apple, Stripe, Nest, Amazon, Robinhood, Safari |
| **Invented** (p. 36) | Coined or derived new word; includes spelling play and domain hacks | Fully ownable; strongest trademark position | Gibberish until meaning is bought/built | Google, Kodak, Spotify, Lyft, Flickr, Hulu |
| **Identity** (p. 37) | Rooted in person, place, or heritage (real or fictionalized) | Human, authentic, story-rich; provenance is power | Can strand you beyond founder/geography; harder to globalize | Patagonia, Tesla, Cisco, Warby Parker, Häagen-Dazs |

Cross-cutting quality worth hunting deliberately — **Special Wrongness** (§3.1, PDF p. 21; term from Ben Pieratt / Peter Mendelsund): an unforgettable newness, an unexpected pairing where 1+1=3, the sense that "in the future, this will be done a lot." Teenage Engineering, Liquid Death, Impossible Foods, The Browser Company of New York.

## Feelings palette (§1.5, PDF p. 12)

Pick ONE dominant feeling during orientation; use it as generation fuel here.

- **Relief** — problem solved, stress gone (Uber, Allegra)
- **Momentum** — accelerating toward goals (Zoom, Stripe)
- **High-Status** — sophistication, success (Tesla, Equinox)
- **Wonder** — curiosity, discovery (Duolingo, Netflix)
- **Rebellion** — rule-breaking, independence (Virgin, Liquid Death)
- **Belonging** — community, being understood (Nextdoor, Etsy)
- **Control** — mastery, precision, command (Ironclad, Console)

## Endings & suffixes (appendix A.3, PDF p. 38)

Useful for tone-setting, and for unlocking trademark/domain viability on a generic word.

- **Corporate**: Partners, Group, Holdings, Alliance, Co., Corp
- **Financial**: Capital, Ventures, Fund, Advisors, Management
- **Creative**: Studio, Collective, Atelier, Works, Productions, Guild
- **Technical**: Labs, Technologies, Systems, Dynamics, Analytics, Robotics
- **Consumer/Retail**: Goods, Market, House, Provisions, Outfitters, Mercantile
- **Medical/Wellness**: Care, Clinic, Health, Life
- **Heritage**: & Sons, Brothers, House of, Heritage

## Exit criteria

A large raw list (agent: 100+; human workshop: 250+), roughly triaged into **Promising / Interesting / Raw** (§2.6, PDF p. 19). If the promising pile is thin, keep generating — do not lower the bar in Distillation to compensate.
