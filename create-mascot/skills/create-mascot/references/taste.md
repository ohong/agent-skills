# Distinct design lenses

Use these lenses to critique original work, not to average several artists into one house style. **Documented** statements summarize attributed sources. **Application** statements are this skill's synthesis. See [sources.md](sources.md) for evidence, credits, and coverage.

## Tolan: relationship determines useful anatomy

**Documented:** The team used embodiment to widen nonverbal expression. Tests changed an evolving pet-like direction into a mature peer. Brows added expression; fingers supported props while making the creature more human. Keyframes and procedural motion worked together. The brand case study shows a broader identity system surrounding the characters. [T1–T3]

**Application:** Decide whether this product needs a pet, peer, guide, performer, or something else. Ask what the neutral face promises about competence and warmth. Add anatomy when it earns expressive value. Keep a coherent shape and material vocabulary across related assets, without adopting Tolan's palette, alien species, planet, or relationship model.

Tolan's credits differ by contribution: Lucas Zanotto and Eran Hilleli provided creative direction; Hilleli and Finn Ericson/Iorama developed characters; Chromosphere and Laura Sirvent contributed animation. David McGillivray's case study covers brand and digital design. Do not credit the whole product to one contributor.

## Lucas Zanotto: a simple mechanism makes personality legible

**Documented:** Zanotto describes reducing forms, observing mechanisms, sketching interacting shapes, and developing motion from those relationships. He values continuity across physical and digital media. His accounts distinguish carefully closed loops from small imperfections that preserve physical feeling. Particular works connect excitement with swinging/release or satisfaction with an unlikely successful shot. [Z1–Z6]

**Observed:** Sampled portfolio loops use simple volumes, structural facial features, readable eyes, contact shadows, and actions such as balancing or compression. In *Having A Face*, small eyes change how existing objects read. These are examples, not a compulsory palette or facial recipe. [Z7–Z9]

**Application:** Give each concept a physical verb before decoration. Make form do several jobs. Let an emotion emerge from anticipation and a consequence. Design the return as carefully as the action. Use controlled asymmetry to convey material, weight, or effort rather than random jitter.

## Hornet: choose craft for this character and story

**Documented:** Hornet represents different directors and media. Yves Geleyn's interviews connect research, construction tests, and economical acting. *Scarecrow* develops a discarded character through dance. *Tetley* uses a deliberate medium change as part of its joke. Peter Sluszka's *Grinch Christmas* preserves distinctive lines while making designs efficient to animate. Andrea Love and Phoebe Wahl's *Tulip* uses a coherent tactile world. [H1–H8]

**Observed:** *Scarecrow* stills make long-limbed poses readable through separation and a quiet setting. A *Tulip* still communicates tenderness through wool texture, body orientation, and contact with a bird. Neither observation establishes film timing.

**Application:** Test what posture communicates before the face is readable. Let material predict movement, or name a meaningful exception. Choose graphic, tactile, rigid, soft, or mixed construction because it serves the idea. Do not reduce this director roster to rounded pastel CG or attribute one director's tastes to all of Hornet.

## Chromosphere: preserve the graphic picture through production

**Documented:** The studio's process connects personality, boards, timed animatics, turnarounds, color, materials, and acting. *June* balances reduced detail with subtle performance. *Yuki 7* adapts rigs for its target engine and uses deliberately graphic geometry; its technical choices protect specific silhouettes and compositions. *Playdate* was animated at the device's real resolution. [C1–C7]

**Application:** Treat the selected drawing and expression as benchmarks throughout modeling and rendering. Define allowed cameras before using view-specific cheats. Build the difficult pose or overlap early. Judge small-scale legibility on the actual intended background. Test the final material and renderer together when a runtime is in scope.

Chromosphere is a collaborative studio. Credit project practitioners where the source names them. Its detailed *Yuki 7* accounts are strong production evidence, but that project's angular, imperfect treatment is not a universal style. Its frame-by-frame rendered effects do not establish browser performance.

## John Bai's Grok Bot workflow: compare real behavior on one canonical asset

**Documented:** Distinct experimental, motion, design, and engineering roles developed alternatives. The motion playground used the production asset with adjustable timing and spring parameters. Comparative feedback and structured design data helped keep repeated output consistent. [B1]

**Application:** Make alternatives cheap and concrete. After selection, retain one canonical editable asset and tune it against the same motion beat and camera. Expose a small set of understandable controls. Do not interpret a particular team's workflow as mandatory agents, Figma usage, or a reason to regenerate the character on every iteration.

## Resolve tensions against the brief

- Reduction versus expression: remove detail until a required emotion stops reading; restore the smallest effective feature or control.
- Restraint versus exuberance: a task guide needs quiet intervals; a performer may legitimately make dance the point.
- Consistent material versus transformation: establish physical rules, then use exceptions deliberately rather than as random effects.
- View-specific art versus free interaction: only use camera cheats inside the allowed viewing range.
- Novel technique versus simple production: complexity should buy a visible or interactive result.

The sources do not establish universal browser budgets, accessibility behavior, exact emotion mappings, or a common list of artist dislikes. Those are implementation decisions, documented and tested separately.
