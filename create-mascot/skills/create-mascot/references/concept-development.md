# Develop concepts through performance

## Establish a useful brief

Infer what the project already establishes: audience, tone, palette, typography, surface, and intended size. Clarify consequential gaps together, especially the mascot's role and its relationship to the user. A serious financial tool, a playful learning site, and a reflective companion need different behavior. Do not let references decide that relationship accidentally.

Identify where the mascot appears and which real events deserve a response. Include a quiet state. Separate communicating product status from entertainment or companionship. For example, a searching pose may communicate active work; it must not imply that an idle service is doing something.

## Compare two starting approaches

A look-first approach quickly produces attractive imagery and can help when an established design needs polish. Its risk is a beautiful still with no distinct behavior or workable anatomy.

A performance-led approach starts with an outline and physical verb, then tests expressions. It exposes weak concepts before modeling. Prefer it for new mascots; use imagery to make the resulting alternatives easy to judge.

Develop about three candidates unless the brief or an existing selection makes that unnecessary. Give each a different organizing idea. A folding creature, a balanced assembly, and a stretching ribbon could express curiosity differently. These are examples of variation, not an anatomy menu.

For each candidate, make the following visible without forcing a fixed presentation schema:

- A recognizable outline and a short explanation of why it fits this product.
- A characteristic physical action, with a beginning, readable change, and return to rest.
- Three distinguishable expressions or poses, plus a useful neutral appearance.
- How gaze, face, and posture create those expressions, including alternatives when there is no conventional face.
- Which features carry identity and which construction or deformation choices need testing.

Concept images should use consistent framing and enough views to explain the design. An expression strip or rough pose sequence is often more useful than a polished hero scene. Generate only the views needed to compare or resolve uncertainty. Do not turn an artist's name into the whole prompt: describe the original form, material, expression, and action explicitly.

Prefer the built-in image-generation tool when available; read its current invocation and reference-image instructions. Adapt this compact prompt recipe rather than prescribing one style:

> Design an original mascot for [product and user relationship]. Use [distinct silhouette and proportions], [face or attention mechanism], and [material/graphic treatment]. Its signature action is [physical verb]. Show the same character in a neutral view and an expression row for [three relevant states], with consistent anatomy, palette, camera, and simple background. Make expression work through gaze and posture. Preserve [identity anchors]; avoid unrequested accessories.

Use a different structural idea for each candidate. For revisions, reference the selected image through the tool's supported mechanism and state what must remain stable. An attractive expression sheet is still a concept, not a tested animation.

## Evaluate and select

Judge recognition at the intended small size, neutral usefulness, emotional range, distinctive movement, brand fit, and feasible construction. Test expressions with body pose and gaze, not only mouth swaps. A mascot need not have every facial feature, limbs, fingers, speech, or props.

Make the recommendation and tradeoffs clear, then obtain a selected direction before expensive production. A prior explicit choice is sufficient. If the user explicitly delegates selection, choose, show the direction and rationale, and proceed without another approval. If they request revisions, change the relevant options. Silence or a vague favorable reaction does not identify a selected direction or delegate the choice.

Preserve a compact record of the chosen silhouette, proportions, face placement, material behavior, signature movement, and critical views. Keep it with the working asset, preferably in Blender's internal text and packed reference image. During modeling, compare the same camera and poses so improvements do not hide a drifting identity.
